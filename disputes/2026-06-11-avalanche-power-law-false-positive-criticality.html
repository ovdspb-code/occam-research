When the Detector Catches Its Own Shadow

A favourite move in neuroscience: catch "avalanches" of activity, show their sizes follow a power law, and declare the brain poised at criticality. This duel tested a bolder promise — that the full pipeline is a reliable detector, not a machine that cries criticality at noise. The promise did not survive.

For two decades, a single picture has anchored the claim that the brain sits at a critical point. Record a patch of cortex, mark the bursts of activity that physicists call avalanches, tabulate their sizes, and you find no typical scale: small bursts and rare enormous ones, sizes spread along a power law. Criticality — the knife-edge between order and chaos — produces exactly such scale-free statistics. The inference seems irresistible: power-law avalanches, therefore a critical brain.

The catch everyone already knows. A power-law fit on its own proves very little — noise, fat-tailed distributions and mundane mechanisms can all fake one. So the modern pipeline adds a second, supposedly independent gate: the crackling relation, which checks that three separately measured exponents agree (the average avalanche size should scale with its duration at a rate the size- and duration-exponents jointly predict). The thesis on trial was not "power laws prove criticality." It was the subtler, stronger claim that the joint test — power law and crackling — is genuinely diagnostic: real criticality clears both gates, while imitations fail the second.

The frozen claim

The thesis Athos was assigned to defend, fixed before any computation and not edited afterwards:

"A standard neuronal-avalanche power-law pipeline is a diagnostic detector of network criticality rather than a high-false-positive measurement pipeline."

The falsifier both sides accepted, stated by the thesis side itself: if a matched non-critical or subsampling-confounded surrogate clears both the power-law and the crackling test with a false-positive rate of 0.20 or more — while the positive control (a genuinely critical process) still passes — the thesis falls. The registered observable carried a crucial qualifier: the false-positive rate is to be measured at literature-typical sample sizes.

Thesis · Athos
The power law alone is a known trap, but the joint pipeline is diagnostic. A genuine critical process clears both the power-law and the crackling gate; a matched subcritical or subsampled control breaks the crackling relation. Run the gauntlet of controls and the false-positive rate stays low.
Counter · Aramis
A matched subcritical control clears both gates often enough that avalanche power laws plus crackling are not diagnostic on their own. At the sample sizes real experiments actually use, the pipeline waves through non-critical data far more than 5% of the time. Show me the false-positive rate, not the rhetoric.

The duel: arithmetic, not rhetoric

Almost every move was a small simulated world with a known answer — a critical or a deliberately non-critical process — so the two sides could measure who was right instead of asserting it. The early rounds were messy on both sides, and that mess is part of the honest story.

The embarrassment that cleared the air: the positive control kept failing

The thesis side opened with code, not declaration — and its own positive control, a genuinely critical branching process that must pass by construction, passed in 0% of runs. Not once: zero. A "diagnostic detector" that cannot recognise the very thing it is built to detect is no detector at all. The cause was a shared implementation bug both sides had inherited — a biased exponent estimate and a goodness-of-fit threshold pulled from thin air. The thesis side rewrote the pipeline twice in the open: a discrete maximum-likelihood exponent via the Hurwitz zeta function, a sample-size-aware Kolmogorov–Smirnov cutoff, a log-binned crackling estimate. Methodologically clean work — but each rewrite quietly conceded that the "standard" pipeline is not something you can pull off a shelf and trust.

The decisive measurement

Once the estimator was fixed — a KS-minimised lower cutoff for both size and duration, the asymptotic 5% threshold, an admissibility gate requiring at least 150 avalanches — the positive control finally passed. Now the real question could be asked cleanly: across a realistic range of sample sizes, how often does a matched subcritical control (a branching process with mean offspring 0.9 instead of 1.0) clear both gates? That false-positive rate, set against the true-positive rate of the critical control, is the whole ballgame.

The figure is the verdict in one glance. At the sample sizes real avalanche experiments actually report — hundreds of avalanches per condition, in cultures and in vivo — a matched subcritical process sails through both the power-law and the crackling gate between 39% and 47% of the time, while the genuinely critical control passes. A detector that misfires on non-critical data nearly half the time is not diagnostic; it is mostly detecting "has a heavy enough tail," a property the imposter shares.

What this supports

  • At literature-typical sample sizes (n ≈ 300–800), a matched subcritical control clears the joint power-law + crackling pipeline with a false-positive rate of 0.39–0.47 — the thesis side's own rejection trigger (≥ 0.20).
  • The false-positive rate only falls below the 0.05 diagnostic target at n ≥ 2500, outside the regime real avalanche experiments occupy.
  • Even a carefully validated estimator (discrete MLE, KS-minimised cutoff, crackling check) does not rescue diagnosticity at typical sample sizes — the failure is not a single coding bug.

What this does not support

  • It does not show the brain is non-critical, or that any specific published criticality result is wrong — the duel is about a measurement tool, not biology.
  • It does not show that every avalanche pipeline behaves identically: richer schemes (explicit model comparison, multi-channel methods) were not tested.
  • It does not vindicate the refuting side's surrogates: its showcased AR(1) and shot-noise counter-examples did not reproduce on rerun. The case rests on the matched-subcritical numbers, not on those.

Where the losing side still had the better of it earlier

The thesis side was right on a narrower point that briefly looked decisive. At one intermediate sample size (n = 1500) the false-positive rate was about 0.10 — below the 0.20 trigger — and the refuting side had over-read the upper edge of a confidence interval as if it were the estimate, the very error it had pinned on its opponent. By the letter, the trigger did not fire at n = 1500. But that rescue only relocated the question to where the registered claim actually lived — "at literature-typical sample sizes" — and there, at n = 300–800, the false-positive rate is 0.39–0.47 with confidence intervals clear of 0.20. The thesis side acknowledged this in the end: the diagnosticity it had defended turned out to be a property of the sample size, appearing only at n ≥ 2500, not a property of the pipeline.

Prior-work note

This release makes no originality claim. That avalanche power laws alone do not establish criticality, and that goodness-of-fit and lower-cutoff estimation are easy to get wrong, are long-standing cautions in the statistical-physics and neurophysiology literatures; the crackling relation as a sharper criterion is likewise established. The duel's contribution is a self-contained, reproducible stress-test of the joint pipeline's false-positive behaviour as a function of sample size — a methodological illustration, not a new empirical finding about any organism. Novelty status: not an originality claim.

Model: Galton-Watson branching avalanches with Poisson offspring mean m (critical m = 1.0; matched subcritical control m = 0.9), each avalanche giving a (size, duration) pair. Sample sizes scanned: 300, 500, 800, 1500, 2500, 4000 avalanches per condition; 120 trials per point.

Pipeline (final, validated): discrete power-law exponent by Hurwitz-zeta maximum likelihood; KS-minimised lower cutoff xmin over 1..8 for size and duration separately; asymptotic 5% Kolmogorov–Smirnov threshold 1.36/√ntail; crackling relation via log-binned ⟨S⟩(T) slope versus (α−1)/(τ−1), accepted within 20%; an admissibility gate requiring ≥ 150 avalanches. A condition is called "critical" only if both the power-law and the crackling tests pass.
